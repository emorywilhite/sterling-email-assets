It's not that your people are dishonest. It's that you're the boss.

Everyone who works for you has to decide whether the hard thing is worth saying. Most days they decide it's not. Not this week. Not with everything else going on.

The people you pay have the same problem. Tell a client what they hoped to hear and you get called back. Tell them what they didn't want to hear and sometimes you don't.

So it gets quiet.

Not all at once. A little more each year you sit in that chair.

You end up knowing more than anyone about your work and your numbers. And less than almost anyone about how your own place really runs.

There's one way I know to get it back.

Four Sterling examiners spend four or five days inside your organization. Not one of them works for you. Not one of them reports to you. None of them will be here next month.

They talk to your leaders. They talk to your managers. They talk to your people.

Then they write it down and hand it to you. What's strong. What's not. What to fix first.

Fulton County Schools did this. Gonzalo La Cava, their Chief Human Resource Officer, came away with the real lesson: great organizations aren't built by simply finding extraordinary people. They're built by creating systems that let people do extraordinary work.

Now let me tell you what this isn't.

It's not comfortable. Four or five days of strangers asking your people hard questions. Then a written report you can't un-read.
